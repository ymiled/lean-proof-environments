\section*{The number I wanted to check}

Theorem's \texttt{lf-lean} write-up reports that when proofs are produced without
compositional structure, success falls by ``roughly a 3x reduction in successful
problem count for every additional dependency solved,'' and that a monolithic
approach ``would yield less than 20\% success.'' That constant is doing real
work. It is the reason to supply already-verified dependencies as trusted
interfaces instead of re-proving them, which is the central architectural choice
of their pipeline.

It was measured once, on one corpus, with one pipeline, and as far as I can tell
nobody has checked it since. So I built an environment to check it, and this note
is mostly about what I learned building it rather than about the number, because
the number turned out not to mean what I wanted it to mean.

\section*{How the environment works}

A \emph{ladder} is a Lean theory plus a dependency graph of lemmas over it. Depth
is computed from the graph rather than written down: a lemma with no dependencies
has depth 1, and otherwise its depth is one more than the deepest thing it needs.
That sounds pedantic and it is not. If depths are declared by hand they drift, and
I will come back to how badly.

A task is a set of lemmas to prove, with everything else in the family supplied
already proved. Two conditions fall out of that. \emph{Compositional} asks for one
lemma with all its ancestors handed over. \emph{Monolithic} asks for that lemma
plus every ancestor, with nothing supplied. At depth five in the arithmetic ladder
that is the difference between writing three lines and writing eight lemmas from
scratch.

Grading runs the Lean kernel. Acceptance needs three things: the file compiles,
\texttt{\#print axioms} on every target stays inside
\{\texttt{propext}, \texttt{Classical.choice}, \texttt{Quot.sound}\}, and the
model's text contains no banned tactic.

The axiom check is the one that matters, and I did not appreciate why until it
caught something. A lemma failed to prove. Lean reported the error and then
\emph{declared the theorem anyway}. Every downstream lemma citing it compiled with
exit status 0 and no diagnostics of its own. Three rungs later the only trace was
\texttt{'L07' depends on axioms: [propext, sorryAx]}. A grader that asks whether
the file compiled would have marked that entire subtree as proved. Exit status is
not a reward signal.

One more design choice worth stating: the model never writes the theorem
statement. The harness emits the \texttt{theorem \ldots := by} header and splices
in only a tactic block. That makes it structurally impossible to weaken or restate
the goal, which removes the largest class of reward hacking without any
statement-comparison code. I had written that comparison code first and deleted
it.

\section*{Where the lemmas come from}

Two of the three families are theories I wrote for the purpose: a Peano
development, and a small security type system. The third is not.

As an undergrad I built a security type system in OCaml, Volpano-Smith-Irvine
style with subtyping, plus a function extension of my own, and proved
noninterference by hand in a paper. For this project I machine-checked that
theorem in Lean 4: 784 lines, no \texttt{sorry}, axioms limited to
\texttt{propext} and \texttt{Quot.sound}. Semantics is fuel-indexed because the
language has while loops, so the theorem is termination-insensitive, relating two
runs that both finish. Expression agreement and command noninterference have to be
proved simultaneously by induction on fuel, since a call inside an expression runs
a command and an assignment inside a command evaluates an expression.

The reason that matters here is not the theorem, which is textbook. It is that the
lemma chain of a real metatheorem makes a much better ladder than anything I would
design. Its depths are the dependency structure the proof actually has, so nobody
can say the ladder was shaped to produce a curve. And it appears in no training
corpus, which the Peano ladder certainly does.

\section*{What the measurements say, and what they do not}

On the arithmetic ladder, monolithic pass rates for Haiku 4.5 fall
$1.00, 0.75, 0.33, 0.00, 0.00$ across depths one to five. Fitting the log of the
pass rate against depth on the non-zero points gives $1.73\times$ per depth with
$R^2 = 0.93$, against the reported $3\times$.

I do not think that is a replication, and the reason is the most useful thing I
found. Refitting the identical data against the \emph{number of lemmas} instead of
depth gives $1.44\times$ per lemma at $R^2 = 0.930$, the same to three decimals.
The two explanations are statistically indistinguishable on this data. Across
these ladders the correlation between depth and total monolithic proof length is
about $0.98$, so any depth curve is also a length curve.

That is not a hypothetical objection. The same \texttt{lf-lean} write-up reports a
difficulty cliff at ``17+ marginal LoC.'' In my arithmetic ladder, monolithic
length crosses seventeen lines exactly between depth three and depth four. Their
own length cliff predicts my curve with no depth effect existing at all. So the
honest reading is that this style of measurement cannot tell you whether chains
compound or whether there is simply more to write.

On the ladder taken from the machine-checked metatheorem, with one frontier model
answering one-shot and with no compiler access:

depth & compositional & $n$ & monolithic & $n$ \\

Supplying the dependencies keeps success between a half and one at every depth.
Withholding them drops it to zero from depth two upward. The depth-five
compositional cell sitting at $1.00$ is not noise: that theorem is a one-line
application of the lemma directly beneath it, which the compositional condition
hands over. Depth and difficulty come apart exactly when the interfaces are
supplied, which is what compositional verification is supposed to buy.

I would not fit a constant to that table. Cells run from two to twelve samples, an
API limit cut the sweep off at fifty of a hundred and forty tasks, and five of the
nine cells fall short of the five samples I was aiming for.

\section*{The benchmark measures its own noise for free}

A lemma at depth one has no ancestors, so its monolithic and compositional prompts
are byte-identical. I checked with \texttt{diff}. They are still answered by
independent model instances under different labels.

In one sweep those identical prompts scored 8/8 in one arm and 6/8 in the other.
Two lemmas passed in one instance and failed in another on the same input, which
puts run-to-run variance somewhere near twenty-five percent. That kills the
apparent depth-one gap in that sweep, and it means every single-sample cell
anywhere in this project has to be read against a quarter-scale coin flip. It also
means depth-one rungs work as a built-in replication control, which I now think
any benchmark of this shape should have deliberately.

\section*{Four things that went wrong}

These cost the most time and are the least likely to be rediscovered by anyone
reading a clean repository, so they are the part I would actually want reviewed.

\textbf{Closed goals collapse depth silently.} My first ladder stated its lemmas
at a concrete point, something like $\mathrm{op}_i\,1\,y = 1$. Every resulting goal
was a closed term, so \texttt{simp} did not reason about it, it evaluated it. A
depth-three rung fell to \texttt{simp\_all} with no lemmas in scope at all. The
ladder compiled, looked plausible, and measured nothing. Rungs have to quantify
over the variables the definitions recurse on.

\textbf{Uniformity and real depth are in conflict.} To compare a chain of $k$
lemmas against $k$ independent ones you want the two arms matched exactly, so I
built a family where every rung had the same shape. It failed, because when each
rung is the previous one composed once more, the chain can be bypassed by
unfolding the definitions and reusing the base lemma. I verified that by compiling
the depth-four lemma with the two beneath it deleted. Matching by construction
requires uniformity, and uniformity is exactly what makes a chain decorative. So
matched pairs have to be searched for among naturally occurring lemmas instead.

\textbf{A funnel admits no independent sets.} Withheld sets have to be closed
upwards, because a supplied lemma cites its own dependencies. That means
independent sets can only be drawn from lemmas nothing depends on. My security
family had exactly one such lemma, the top theorem, so the comparison I wanted was
not underpowered there, it was impossible. Shape of the graph decides which
experiments a ladder can support.

\textbf{A check that cannot fail is worse than no check.} The self-test verifies
that each declared dependency is real by rebuilding the rung without it. For
multi-lemma tasks that check was inert: it graded a monolithic task using the
single-lemma solution, so the missing blocks were filled with \texttt{sorry}, the
axiom audit rejected the attempt for the wrong reason, and the failure branch
could never be reached. It reported green for the entire life of one family, while
four of that family's fourteen lemmas declared dependencies their proofs never
cite. Depths were inflated and every depth-indexed number from it was an artifact.
I found it because one dependency looked implausible on inspection: a one-line
proof cannot need a lemma. The fix was to compile each rung with its ancestors
absent from the file entirely, and to derive the dependency graph from the proof
text rather than a hand-written table.

\section*{What I think the actual finding is}

The grader worked. The dependency machinery worked. The renaming worked. The
certifications worked once repaired. None of those was the bottleneck.

The bottleneck was difficulty calibration. The arithmetic ladder ceilings for a
strong model, so it measures nothing. The first version of the security ladder
floored above depth one, so it measured nothing either. Only a weaker model, or a
rebuilt ladder with intermediate lemmas inserted, landed in the band where there
is a curve to see at all.

A task that always passes and a task that always fails both produce zero gradient.
That is the same sparse-reward complaint usually made about unit tests. So dense
feedback is not a property of proof assistants. It is a property of a task
distribution matched to a particular policy. Proof assistants make dense feedback
possible and do not make it automatic, and anyone planning to scale reinforcement
learning on verification needs a calibration story rather than just a kernel.

\section*{What this is not}

There is no training here. No gradients, no policy updates. This is the
environment half of a reinforcement learning setup with a frozen model in the
policy slot: state space, action space, a task distribution with a difficulty
knob, and a reward that is a proof kernel rather than a learned proxy. There is a
\texttt{reset}/\texttt{step} interface so a learner could be attached. None has
been.

Episodes are single-step: one attempt, one whole proof, one binary reward. Per
tactic actions with intermediate proof states would need a persistent Lean server
instead of one-shot compilation.

And the causal question is still open. The comparison designed to settle it, $k$
chained lemmas against $k$ independent ones at matched length, has only ever been
run against a model that ceilings on it.
